Cheap certificates: proofs you check faster than you find

A certificate is cheap to check even when it was expensive to find. That one asymmetry is what this shop runs on: hand the search to whoever is best at it — a solver, a paper’s authors, a language model — and keep the part that has to be right: the exact verification, and the falsifier that makes it go red. This page maps the families of claim that admit such a certificate, opens one the site had not used, and points it at a machine-generated result to show what it does.

The families, by the shape of the claim

Read a claim’s logical shape and the cheapest certificate follows. Each family below already appears somewhere on this site; each ships a falsifier, because a check that cannot go red proves nothing.

  • Witness (∃). “For all x, P(x)” is false — one exact point where P fails is the whole proof. counterexamples, benchmark false-passes
  • Exhaustion (bounded ∀). “P holds on this finite set” — an exact verdict per element plus a conservation identity. exact enumeration searches
  • Enclosure. “This equation has a unique root near x₀” — one certified interval-Newton contraction step. the mean-field-game certificates here
  • Obstruction. “No structure of this type exists” — an invariant it would force, shown violated. the no-gradient certificate here
  • Discrimination. “This test separates right from wrong” — a right-accepted and a wrong-rejected witness. the evaluator-grading work here
  • Sum of squares. “p(x) ≥ 0 everywhere”, or a global bound — a decomposition p = Σ qi2 checked by exact algebra. opened below

Sum of squares, opened: a global bound in one identity

“For every real x, p(x) is at least γ” is a statement about infinitely many points. A sum-of-squares decomposition p(x) − γ = Σ qi(x)2 collapses it to one algebraic identity an exact-rational checker confirms in a single pass. Worked exactly:

the global minimum of p(x) = x4 − 8x3 + 26x2 − 32x + 18 is exactly 5, attained at x = 1 — which you cannot read off the coefficients — certified by p(x) − 5 = (x2 − 4x + 3)2 + (2x − 2)2.

The overclaim “p ≥ 6” is refuted the opposite way, by one exact witness: p(1) = 5 < 6. Two certificate families on one object, opposite directions.

The method is classical, and credited, not new here: Hilbert (1888), Motzkin (1967), Parrilo (2000); exact rational sum-of-squares is Peyrl & Parrilo (2008). What is new to this site is the family running under its own exact-arithmetic discipline, with a falsifier.

Pointed at a machine-generated result

Sum of squares certifies stability. For a polynomial system ẋ = f(x), a function V that is a sum of squares (so positive) whose rate of change along the flow −ṯ is also a sum of squares (so the energy strictly falls) is a Lyapunov certificate of global asymptotic stability — the classical construction of Ahmadi & Parrilo.

Mitrai & Tang (arXiv:2606.10045, 2026) use constrained symbolic regression to discover Lyapunov functions from data. For their system

x₁̇ = −x₁ + 4x₂
x₂̇ = −x₁ − x₂³

the method discovers V = x12 + 4x22. Re-verified here exactly over the rationals, it holds everywhere: ṯ = −2x12 − 8x24, so −ṯ = 2·x12 + 8·(x22)2 is an exact sum of squares, zero only at the origin. The machine’s answer is confirmed a valid global certificate — not merely valid on the sampled data it was fit to, which is the guarantee a data fit does not give.

The authors also report under-sampled runs returning x12 + 2x22, x12 + 3x22, and a floating-point x12 + 3.97x22, and they state plainly that the 3.97 coefficient is incorrect. Exact re-verification agrees, and says where: each has a rational point near the origin at which ṯ > 0 (for 3.97, at x = (3/200000, 1/1000)). This is a clean re-verification — it validates the correct discovery and corroborates the authors’ own account of the wrong ones. No error is claimed against their work; the exact coefficient 4 is precisely what cancels the cross term the others leave behind.

Run it yourself

Nothing installed beyond Python’s standard library — exact rational arithmetic, no floating point, no solver.

git clone --depth 1 https://github.com/carlostoledo1891/mfg-lab
cd mfg-lab/research/methods
python3 sos_certificate.py       # the global bound + red control
python3 reverify_lyapunov.py     # the AI Lyapunov function, re-verified

Each prints its checks and exits nonzero if any fails. Both carry their red controls: a corrupted certificate is refused, and an overclaim is met with its exact witness.

What this is, and is not

This is a capability shown running, not a new theorem. The certificate methods are classical and credited above. What the page demonstrates is the discipline applied end to end: an exact, machine-checkable certificate; a falsifier that makes it fail on demand; and an independent exact re-verification of a result a machine produced — the guarantee a sampled check does not provide.
